SEMINAR 1 · 10:00 – 11:00

Life at the Extremes in the Anthropocene

From the North Pole to Vietnam: biodiversity, ecosystem services and environmental intelligence

Dr. Khuong V. Dinh
Principal Investigator & Senior Lecturer
Department of Biosciences, University of Oslo, Norway
Photo: North Pole, 31 July 2026 · Vegard Stürzinger

Abstract

From Arctic seas to tropical rainforests, rivers, and lakes, natural ecosystems sustain biodiversity and provide essential ecological services, including climate regulation, cleaner water, floodwater storage, disease regulation, and food and medicine resources. Vietnam’s forests alone have been valued at ~US$35.6b annually in ecosystem-service benefits. Marine copepods sustain aquatic food webs and provide nutritious live feed for marine fish larvae, supporting fisheries and aquaculture within Vietnam’s seafood sector, which generated ~US$11.3b in exports in 2025. Yet climate change and human activities threaten the ecological processes sustaining these benefits.

From the North Pole and Arctic polar nights to Vietnam’s tropical waters, my research examines how warming and pollution affect species, ecosystem functioning, and human well-being. Across these environments, resilience depends on interactions between environmental pressures, organisms’ adaptations, seasonal rhythms, and exposure histories, with consequences extending across seasons and generations.

Building on these insights, I present a framework for Vietnam connecting biodiversity discovery and long-term monitoring with ecological experiments, genomics, and forecasting. By identifying vulnerable species and habitats, assessing adaptive capacity, and evaluating interventions, this framework can inform conservation and restoration. For environmentally friendly housing and smart cities, it can guide species selection and the design of urban lakes, wetlands, and green spaces to help manage heat, water quality, and flooding. Environmental DNA, sensors, remote sensing, and artificial intelligence can support these decisions and evaluate outcomes.

Drawing on research and leadership experience within the Nansen Legacy (2018–2024; ~$79m) and Polhavet 2050 (Arctic Ocean 2050, 2026–2035; ~US$214m), I conclude by exploring coordinated research opportunities linking biodiversity science with technological innovation to strengthen Vietnam’s scientific leadership and support climate-resilient communities.

About the speaker

Dr. Khuong V. Dinh is an aquatic ecologist, Principal Investigator, and Senior Lecturer in the Department of Biosciences at the University of Oslo, Norway. His research examines how climate change and pollution interact to affect aquatic organisms, biodiversity, and ecosystem resilience. Working across tropical and Arctic environments, he focuses on thermal adaptation, transgenerational effects, and the vulnerability of animals to multiple environmental stressors.

He leads a Research Council of Norway Young Research Talent project investigating how Arctic zooplankton survive the challenges of overwintering in a changing ocean. His experience includes scientific leadership in several national programs and research expeditions to the Barents Sea, central Arctic Ocean, and North Pole.

Dr. Dinh has authored or co-authored 79 scientific articles and book chapters and collaborated with more than 200 scientists and students internationally. Alongside his research, he is committed to teaching, mentoring early career researchers, and communicating science to wider audiences. He is a Fellow of the Royal Society of Biology, UK.

SEMINAR 2 · 11:30 – 12:30

Application of molecular approaches to discovering and conserving Vietnam’s biodiversity

Unlocking and safeguarding the country’s rich natural heritage

Dr. Minh D. Le
Head, Genomic Analysis and Conservation (GAC) Excellence Research Group
VNU University of Science and VNU Central Institute for Natural Resources and Environmental Studies

Abstract

Situated within the Indo-Burma Biodiversity Hotspot, Vietnam is widely recognized as one of the world’s most biodiverse countries. This presentation will highlight several key research directions pursued by members of GAC and their collaborators over the past two decades to uncover and protect the country’s biodiversity. Since the early 2000s, the integration of molecular tools into taxonomic research has helped resolve the taxonomic status of numerous cryptic species complexes, leading to the discovery and description of hundreds of new species. This trend is expected to continue well into the future as molecular approaches gain broader acceptance and research capacity expands through the establishment of additional laboratories across the country.

In recent years, genetic methods have also been applied to resolving species conservation issues in Vietnam. Research has been conducted to identify the distributions of some of the most threatened species in the world, such as the Swinhoe’s giant softshell turtle (Rafetus swinhoei), the Vietnamese pond turtle (Mauremys annamensis), and the Saola (Pseudoryx nghetinhensis). Genetic and health screenings have been undertaken for various endangered species to assist efforts to reintroduce confiscated animals back to their native habitats. Genomic approaches have also been utilized to gain deeper insights into the genetic makeup and ecological adaptations of highly threatened species, thereby informing long-term conservation strategies. In the coming years, molecular tools are expected to play an increasingly important role in addressing a broad range of questions, from taxonomy and the evolutionary history of biota to the conservation of threatened wildlife species in this global biodiversity hotspot.

About the speaker

Minh D. Le has been working on conservation-related issues in Southeast Asia for more than 20 years. His work focuses on developing science-based innovative conservation measures for highly threatened species in Indochina. Minh is currently working on projects that attempt to conserve some of the most endangered species in Vietnam, such as the Swinhoe’s giant softshell turtle (Rùa hoàn kiếm), Saola, the Vietnamese pond turtle (Rùa trung bộ), and the large-antlered muntjac (Mang lớn), by implementing various conservation actions, including payment for ecosystem services, SMART training for local personnel, raising stakeholders’ awareness of conservation issues, and improving local livelihoods. He has also pioneered the application of molecular tools and other conservation technologies to support the protection of critically endangered species in Vietnam. Along with collaborators, he has published more than 200 scientific papers and books on issues related to conservation, ecology, evolution, and taxonomy.
